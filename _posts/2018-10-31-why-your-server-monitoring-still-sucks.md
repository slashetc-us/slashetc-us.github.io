<p><em>Five observations about why your your server monitoring still<br />
stinks by a monitoring specialist-turned-consultant.</em></p>
<p>
Early in my career, I was responsible for managing a large fleet of<br />
printers across a large campus. We're talking several hundred networked<br />
printers. It often required a 10- or 15-minute walk to get to<br />
some of those printers physically, and many were used only sporadically. I<br />
didn't<br />
always know what was happening until I arrived, so it was anyone's<br />
guess as to the problem. Simple paper jam? Driver issue? Printer currently<br />
on fire? I found out only after the long walk. Making this even more<br />
frustrating for everyone was that, thanks to the infrequent use of some of<br />
them, a printer with a problem might go unnoticed for weeks, making itself<br />
known only when someone tried to print with it.</p>
<p>
Finally, it occurred to me: wouldn't it be nice if I knew about the problem<br />
and the cause <em>before</em> someone called me? I found my first monitoring tool<br />
that day, and I was absolutely hooked.</p>
<p>
Since then, I've helped numerous people overhaul their monitoring<br />
systems. In doing so, I noticed the same challenges repeat themselves regularly. If<br />
you're responsible for managing the systems at your organization, read<br />
on; I have much advice to dispense.</p>
<p>
So, without further ado, here are my top five reasons why your monitoring<br />
is crap and what you can do about it.</p>
<h3>
1. You're Using Antiquated Tools</h3>
<p>
By far, the most common reason for monitoring being screwed up is a<br />
reliance on antiquated tools. You know that's your issue when you spend<br />
too much time working around the warts of your monitoring tools or when<br />
you've got a bunch of custom code to get around some major missing<br />
functionality. But the bottom line is that you spend more time trying to<br />
fix the almost-working tools than just getting on with your job.</p>
<p>
The problem with using antiquated tools and methodologies is that<br />
you're just making it harder for yourself. I suppose it's certainly<br />
possible to dig a hole with a rusty spoon, but wouldn't you prefer to use a<br />
shovel?</p>
<p>
Great tools are invisible. They make you more effective, and the job is<br />
easier to accomplish. When you have great tools, you don't even notice<br />
them.</p>
<p>
Maybe you don't describe your monitoring tools as "easy to use"<br />
or "invisible". The words you might opt to use would make my editor<br />
break out a red pen.</p>
<p>
This checklist can help you determine if you're screwing yourself.</p>
